TRADING ACADEMY • LESSON 10

Trading Psychology

Learn how emotions, discipline and decision-making can influence your trading behavior and how to develop a more consistent process.

1. What Is Trading Psychology?

Trading psychology refers to the mental and emotional factors that influence how a trader makes decisions.

A trader can have a well-designed strategy and still make poor decisions because of fear, greed, impatience or emotional reactions to wins and losses.

Core principle:

Good trading is not only about finding entries. It is also about controlling your behavior before, during and after a trade.

2. The Four Major Emotional Challenges

😨

Fear

Fear can cause hesitation, premature exits or avoidance of valid setups.

🤑

Greed

Greed can encourage oversized positions, unrealistic targets and excessive risk.

🔥

FOMO

Fear of missing out can cause traders to enter after a move has already happened.

🔄

Revenge

Revenge trading happens when a trader attempts to recover losses through emotional decisions.

3. Fear

Fear is a natural response to uncertainty. Trading involves uncertainty because future price movements cannot be known with certainty.

Fear can become a problem when it causes a trader to repeatedly abandon a well-defined plan.

Example

A trader enters a position according to their plan. Price temporarily moves against them.

Instead of following the predetermined risk plan, the trader closes the trade immediately because they become afraid of losing.

The problem is not necessarily the emotion itself. The problem is allowing the emotion to override the trading process.

4. Greed

Greed can appear after both winning and losing trades.

A trader may increase position size because they believe a large profit is coming.

This can expose the account to losses that are much larger than originally planned.

More risk does not guarantee more reward.

Increasing position size increases exposure. It does not improve the certainty of the trade.

5. FOMO — Fear of Missing Out

FOMO occurs when a trader feels pressure to enter a market because price is moving without them.

This often happens after seeing a large candle or a strong price movement.

  • Chasing price after a major move
  • Entering without a complete setup
  • Ignoring the original trading plan
  • Taking trades simply because others are trading

6. Revenge Trading

Revenge trading is an attempt to recover losses quickly through additional trades driven by frustration or anger.

The Dangerous Cycle

Loss → frustration → larger position → another loss → increased frustration → even larger position.

Break the cycle.

After a significant emotional event, stepping away from the market can be more valuable than immediately looking for another trade.

7. Overtrading

Overtrading means taking more trades than your strategy or plan justifies.

More trades do not automatically mean more opportunities for profit.

Every additional trade creates another opportunity for risk, costs and emotional mistakes.

8. Discipline

Discipline means following a predefined process even when emotions encourage you to behave differently.

A disciplined trader does not need to predict every market movement. They need to execute their process consistently.

Discipline beats impulse.

A trading plan is useful only if you can actually follow it.

9. Trading Plan

A trading plan should define the conditions under which you will and will not trade.

Depending on your strategy, it may include:

  • Markets you trade
  • Trading sessions
  • Timeframes
  • Entry conditions
  • Stop-loss rules
  • Position-sizing rules
  • Profit-taking rules
  • Maximum daily or weekly risk
  • Conditions for stopping trading

10. Trading Journal

A trading journal records information about your trades so you can review your decisions over time.

Market: What instrument did you trade?
Setup: Why did you enter?
Entry: Where did you enter?
Stop: Where was your invalidation level?
Target: What was your planned exit?
Risk: How much were you risking?
Emotion: How did you feel before and during the trade?
Result: What happened?
Lesson: What can you improve?

11. Winning Streaks

Winning streaks can also create psychological problems.

After several successful trades, a trader may become overconfident and begin taking excessive risk.

Confidence is not the same as certainty.

A successful trade does not guarantee that the next trade will succeed.

12. Losing Streaks

Losing streaks are possible even when a strategy has a positive long-term expectation.

The important question is whether losses occurred within the rules of the strategy or because the trader broke their own rules.

13. Process Over Outcome

One trade should not determine whether you are a good or bad trader.

A better approach is to evaluate whether you followed your process.

Outcome Focus Process Focus
"I lost, so the trade was bad." "Did I follow my plan?"
"I won, so my decision was perfect." "Was the decision justified?"
"I need to recover today." "I need to protect my risk limits."

14. A Pre-Trade Mental Checklist

  • Am I calm enough to make a rational decision?
  • Does this trade meet my strategy rules?
  • Do I know where the trade is invalidated?
  • Is the risk acceptable?
  • Am I entering because of my plan or because I am afraid of missing the move?
  • Am I trying to recover a previous loss?

15. When You Should Stop Trading

A trader should consider stopping when emotional conditions make disciplined decision-making difficult.

Warning signs can include:

  • Anger after a loss
  • Strong urge to immediately recover money
  • Repeated rule-breaking
  • Unusual position sizes
  • Trading without a clear setup
  • Unable to accept a normal loss

16. Building Consistency

Consistency does not mean winning every day.

It means repeatedly applying the same disciplined process while accepting that individual outcomes are uncertain.

Think in series, not single trades.

Evaluate your strategy over a meaningful sample of trades rather than judging everything from one result.

17. Practical Exercise

The 10-Trade Discipline Challenge

Before taking your next ten demo trades, write down your rules before entering each trade.

After each trade, record whether you followed your rules.

At the end of ten trades, calculate how many trades followed your plan.

The objective is not simply to make money. The objective is to measure your discipline.

18. Common Psychology Mistakes

  • Trading while angry or emotionally unstable.
  • Increasing risk after losses.
  • Taking trades because another trader posted a profit.
  • Changing strategies after a few losing trades.
  • Moving stop-losses because you cannot accept the planned loss.
  • Trading constantly because you feel you must always be in the market.
  • Confusing luck with skill after a winning trade.
