TRADING ACADEMY • LESSON 09

Risk Management

Learn how traders manage potential losses, calculate position size, use stop-losses and protect their trading capital.

1. What Is Risk Management?

Risk management is the process of controlling how much money you could lose if a trade does not go as expected.

It is one of the most important parts of a trading plan because no analysis method can guarantee that every trade will be profitable.

Core principle:

The goal is not to avoid every losing trade. The goal is to prevent individual losses from destroying your trading account.

2. Risk Per Trade

Risk per trade is the amount of your account you are prepared to lose if your trade reaches its planned stop-loss.

Many traders express this as a percentage of account equity.

Risk Amount = Account Balance × Risk Percentage

3. Example: Calculating Risk

Educational Example

Suppose a hypothetical account contains $1,000.

If the trader chooses to risk 1% on one trade:

$1,000 × 0.01 = $10

The planned risk would therefore be $10 before considering trading costs such as spreads, commissions or slippage.

4. Stop-Loss

A stop-loss is an order or predefined exit level designed to limit a trade's loss if price moves against the position.

The exact execution price can differ from the intended level during fast markets or gaps, depending on the instrument and broker.

A stop-loss is not a guarantee of an exact loss.

Slippage and market conditions can cause the actual result to differ from the planned risk.

5. Why Stop-Loss Placement Matters

A stop-loss should be connected to the reason for taking the trade.

Placing a stop extremely close simply to reduce the dollar amount of risk can cause a position to exit from normal market fluctuations.

Instead, traders should first determine where their trade idea would be invalidated and then calculate an appropriate position size.

6. Position Sizing

Position sizing determines how large a trade should be based on the amount of money being risked and the distance to the stop-loss.

The general concept is:

Position Size = Risk Amount ÷ Risk Per Unit

The exact calculation depends on the instrument, contract specifications, account currency, stop distance and broker.

7. Example of Position Sizing

Simplified Example

Suppose your planned risk is $10.

Your chosen position would lose $2 for every unit of movement between the entry and stop-loss.

$10 ÷ $2 = 5 units

This simplified example gives a position size of 5 units.

Real forex position sizing requires the actual pip value or monetary value per price movement for the specific instrument.

8. Risk-to-Reward Ratio

Risk-to-reward compares the amount potentially lost with the amount targeted for a trade.

For example, a 1:2 risk-to-reward setup means the planned potential reward is twice the planned risk.

Risk : Reward = 1 : 2

If the planned risk were $10, the hypothetical target would be $20 before costs.

Important:

A high risk-to-reward ratio does not guarantee profitability. The probability of reaching the target also matters.

9. Win Rate and Risk-to-Reward

Win rate and risk-to-reward should be considered together.

Example Risk Target
1:1 $10 $10
1:2 $10 $20
1:3 $10 $30

These numbers describe planned outcomes, not guaranteed results.

10. Leverage

Leverage allows a trader to control a larger notional position using a smaller amount of margin.

For example, 1:100 leverage means that $1 of margin can support up to $100 of notional exposure, subject to the broker's rules and instrument specifications.

Leverage does not make a trade safer.

Higher leverage can make it possible to open larger positions, which can increase both potential gains and potential losses.

11. Margin

Margin is the amount of account funds required to open and maintain a leveraged position.

Margin is not the same thing as the maximum amount you can afford to lose.

A position can require a relatively small amount of margin while still exposing the account to significant market risk.

12. Drawdown

Drawdown measures a decline from an account's previous peak value.

Example

An account grows from $1,000 to $1,200.

It then falls to $1,080.

The decline from the $1,200 peak is:

($1,200 - $1,080) ÷ $1,200 × 100 = 10%

The account has experienced a 10% drawdown from its peak.

13. Why Large Losses Are Dangerous

The percentage gain required to recover from a loss becomes larger as the loss increases.

Account Loss Gain Needed to Recover
10% 11.11%
20% 25%
30% 42.86%
50% 100%
Protecting capital matters.

A 50% account loss requires a 100% gain on the remaining capital just to return to the starting balance.

14. Losing Streaks

Even a strategy that has historically performed well can experience a sequence of losing trades.

Risk management should therefore account for the possibility of consecutive losses.

Reducing risk can help limit the damage caused by an unexpected losing streak.

15. Small Accounts

Small accounts require particular discipline.

Trying to turn a very small balance into a large amount quickly can encourage excessive leverage, oversized positions and emotional decisions.

Do not confuse account size with acceptable risk.

A small balance does not justify risking an unreasonably large percentage of the account.

16. Trading Costs

Risk calculations should consider costs such as:

  • Spread
  • Commission
  • Swap or overnight financing where applicable
  • Slippage

These costs can affect the actual result of a trade.

17. Risk Management Checklist

  • What is my account equity?
  • How much money am I willing to risk?
  • Where is the trade idea invalidated?
  • Where is the stop-loss?
  • What is the position size?
  • What are the trading costs?
  • What is the planned reward relative to the risk?
  • Can I accept the potential loss without changing my plan emotionally?

18. Common Risk Management Mistakes

  • Risking too much on one trade.
  • Increasing position size after a loss to recover money quickly.
  • Moving a stop-loss farther away because the trade is losing.
  • Using excessive leverage.
  • Ignoring spread, commission or slippage.
  • Trading money needed for essential expenses.
  • Taking multiple highly correlated positions without considering their combined exposure.

19. Practical Exercise

Exercise A

Imagine an account with $500.

You decide, for this educational exercise, to risk 1%.

$500 × 0.01 = $5

Your planned risk is $5.

Exercise B

Imagine an account with $2,000 and a hypothetical risk limit of 0.5%.

$2,000 × 0.005 = $10

The planned risk is $10.
