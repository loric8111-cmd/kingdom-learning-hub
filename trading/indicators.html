Technical Indicators

Learn what technical indicators measure, how common indicators work, and how they can be used as supporting information when studying price charts.

1. What Is a Technical Indicator?

A technical indicator is a mathematical calculation based on market data such as price and, in some cases, volume.

Indicators transform historical market information into a visual or numerical representation that traders can study.

Important:

An indicator does not know the future. It processes available market data and can therefore be wrong.

2. Common Indicators

MA

Moving Average

Helps smooth price data and visualize an average price over a selected period.

RSI

Relative Strength Index

A momentum oscillator commonly used to study the speed and magnitude of recent price movements.

MACD

Moving Average Convergence Divergence

A momentum and trend-following indicator based on moving averages.

ATR

Average True Range

Measures market volatility rather than predicting whether price will rise or fall.

3. Moving Averages

A moving average calculates an average from a selected number of previous price observations.

As new price data appears, older data eventually drops out of the calculation. This causes the average to move with price.

Moving averages can help traders visualize the general direction of historical price movement.

4. Simple Moving Average — SMA

A Simple Moving Average gives equal mathematical weight to the observations included in its period.

Example

A 20-period SMA calculates an average using the selected 20 previous price observations.

The exact value depends on the data and the calculation method used by the charting platform.

5. Exponential Moving Average — EMA

An Exponential Moving Average gives greater weight to more recent price observations.

Because of this weighting, an EMA can respond to recent price changes differently from an SMA.

Indicator Basic Characteristic
SMA Equal weighting within the selected period
EMA Greater weighting toward recent data

6. Moving Average Limitations

Moving averages are calculated from historical data. This means they respond to price rather than predicting price with certainty.

They can also behave differently in trending and ranging markets.

Remember:

A moving average crossing another moving average is not automatically a profitable trading signal.

7. RSI — Relative Strength Index

RSI is a momentum oscillator that commonly uses a scale from 0 to 100.

It is designed to measure the strength of recent price movements.

Traders often study RSI when considering whether recent momentum may be relatively strong or weak.

8. RSI and the 70/30 Levels

A common RSI setup highlights 70 and 30 as reference levels.

  • RSI above 70 is commonly described as "overbought."
  • RSI below 30 is commonly described as "oversold."
But be careful:

Overbought does not automatically mean "sell," and oversold does not automatically mean "buy."

Strong trends can remain at extreme RSI levels for extended periods.

9. MACD

MACD stands for Moving Average Convergence Divergence.

It is commonly used to study momentum and trend-related behavior.

The indicator is built from exponential moving averages and includes a MACD line, signal line and histogram.

10. Understanding MACD Crossovers

Traders sometimes compare the MACD line with the signal line.

When the two lines cross, traders may interpret the event as information about changing momentum.

However, crossovers can produce false signals, especially in sideways markets.

11. ATR — Average True Range

ATR is primarily a volatility indicator.

It helps describe how much an instrument has been moving over a selected period.

ATR does not tell you whether the market will move upward or downward.

Example

If volatility increases, ATR may rise.

If price movement becomes quieter, ATR may fall.

This can help traders understand the changing volatility environment.

12. Leading vs. Lagging Information

Some trading discussions classify indicators as "leading" or "lagging."

Most traditional indicators are calculated from existing market data, so they should not be treated as crystal balls that predict the future.

Concept Meaning
Historical data Information already produced by the market
Lagging behavior Indicator responds to previous price information
Prediction A future outcome that remains uncertain

13. Indicators and Price Action

Indicators can be used alongside the concepts you have already learned.

For example, you might study:

  • Market structure
  • Support and resistance
  • Trendlines
  • Candlestick behavior
  • Momentum indicators
  • Volatility

The purpose is to build a broader picture rather than depend on one indicator.

14. Avoid Indicator Overload

Adding many indicators to a chart does not necessarily improve analysis.

Several indicators can also provide similar information because they are calculated from related price data.

Keep your chart understandable.

Use tools because they answer a specific analytical question—not simply because they look professional.

15. Example of Combining Information

Educational Example

Suppose market structure shows Higher Highs and Higher Lows.

Price approaches a previously identified support zone.

An indicator such as RSI shows that recent downward momentum has weakened.

These observations may provide context for further analysis, but they do not guarantee that price will rise.

16. Common Beginner Mistakes

  • Using indicators without understanding what they calculate.
  • Treating RSI 70 as an automatic sell signal.
  • Treating RSI 30 as an automatic buy signal.
  • Entering every time two moving averages cross.
  • Using too many indicators.
  • Ignoring market structure and price action.
  • Believing indicators can eliminate trading risk.

17. Practical Exercise

Your Task

Open a demo EUR/USD chart.

Add a 20-period SMA and a 50-period SMA.

Then add RSI.

Observe how the indicators behave during an uptrend, a downtrend and a ranging market.

Do not place a real-money trade based solely on this exercise.
