Support & Resistance

Learn how important price areas are identified and how traders use support and resistance when analyzing charts.

1. What Is Support?

Support is a price area where downward movement has previously slowed or where buying interest has appeared.

Traders watch these areas because price may react there again. However, support is not a guarantee that price will stop falling.

Think of support as an area, not a magic line.

Price can move slightly above or below an area before reacting.

2. What Is Resistance?

Resistance is a price area where upward movement has previously slowed or where selling interest has appeared.

Traders may watch the area for another reaction, but resistance can also fail.

3. Support and Resistance Are Zones

Beginners often draw one perfectly precise line and expect price to respect it exactly. Real markets are usually more complicated.

Support and resistance are often better understood as areas where price has previously reacted.

4. How Do You Find Support?

Look at historical price action and identify areas where price repeatedly reacted after moving lower.

Possible clues include:

  • Previous swing lows
  • Repeated reactions from a similar area
  • Consolidation areas
  • Important higher-timeframe price levels

5. How Do You Find Resistance?

Look for areas where price repeatedly struggled to continue higher.

Possible clues include:

  • Previous swing highs
  • Repeated rejection areas
  • Previous consolidation zones
  • Important higher-timeframe levels

6. Example of Support

Example

Imagine EUR/USD falls toward 1.1000 several times.

Each time price approaches the area, selling slows and price moves upward again.

The 1.1000 area could therefore be considered a potential support zone.

It is not guaranteed to hold in the future.

7. Example of Resistance

Example

Imagine EUR/USD rises toward 1.1100 several times.

Each time price approaches the area, upward movement slows and price moves lower.

The 1.1100 area could therefore be considered a potential resistance zone.

8. Role Reversal

Sometimes an area that previously acted as support can later become resistance.

The opposite can also happen: an area that acted as resistance may later become support.

Simple Example

Price repeatedly holds above an area around 1.1000.

Later, price breaks below that area.

If price subsequently rises back toward 1.1000 and struggles there, traders may describe the area as having changed from support to resistance.

9. Breakouts

A breakout occurs when price moves beyond an important price area.

For example, price may move above a resistance zone or below a support zone.

A breakout does not automatically mean that price will continue in the same direction.

10. False Breakouts

Sometimes price moves beyond a support or resistance area and then quickly returns back inside the previous range.

This is often called a false breakout or failed breakout.

Important:

Never assume that every breakout will continue. Wait for the price behavior and broader context to support your analysis.

11. Higher-Timeframe Levels

Support and resistance can be observed on many different timeframes.

A level visible on a daily or weekly chart may provide broader context than a level visible only on a very short timeframe.

This does not mean higher-timeframe levels will always hold. It simply means traders may consider them when building a broader market view.

12. Strong and Weak Levels

Not all price areas have equal significance.

Possible Characteristic What It May Indicate
Multiple reactions The area has attracted attention before.
Clear swing point The area may be structurally important.
Higher-timeframe visibility The area may provide broader context.
Very recent level May reflect current market behavior.

13. Support & Resistance With Market Structure

Support and resistance become more useful when studied together with market structure.

For example, in an upward structure, previous swing areas may become important places to observe how price reacts during a pullback.

In a downward structure, previous swing areas can also provide useful context.

14. Do Not Draw Too Many Levels

A common beginner mistake is covering the chart with dozens of horizontal lines.

Too many levels can make analysis confusing.

Better approach:

Focus on the most meaningful areas and ask why each level matters before putting it on your chart.

15. A Simple Method for Marking Zones

  • Start with a higher timeframe.
  • Identify obvious swing highs and lows.
  • Look for areas where price reacted multiple times.
  • Mark a zone rather than forcing perfect precision.
  • Move to a lower timeframe only after understanding the broader context.
  • Observe what price does when it approaches the zone.

16. Common Beginner Mistakes

  • Treating support and resistance as guaranteed.
  • Drawing too many levels.
  • Ignoring higher timeframes.
  • Entering immediately whenever price touches a level.
  • Assuming every breakout is real.
  • Ignoring market structure.
  • Using levels without a risk-management plan.

17. Risk Management Still Comes First

Finding a good-looking support or resistance zone does not eliminate trading risk.

Price can break through a level unexpectedly. News events can produce rapid movement, and technical analysis can be wrong.

A responsible trading plan should define how much capital you are willing to risk before entering a trade.
